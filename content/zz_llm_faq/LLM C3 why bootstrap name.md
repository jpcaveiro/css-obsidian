The name comes from the English idiom "to pull oneself up by one's own bootstraps" — an impossible feat of lifting yourself off the ground by pulling on your own boot straps. Bradley Efron chose the name in his 1979 paper because the bootstrap does something that seems equally impossible: it extracts the sampling distribution, standard errors, and confidence intervals of a statistic using **only the data you already have**, with no extra samples and no distributional theory — resampling from the sample itself, as if the data were "lifting itself up."

Efron noted the phrase traces back to the 18th-century _Adventures of Baron Munchausen_ by Rudolph Raspe, where the Baron, drowning at the bottom of a lake, saves himself by pulling on his own bootstraps. It's the same metaphor as "booting" a computer — the machine starts itself up from nothing.
