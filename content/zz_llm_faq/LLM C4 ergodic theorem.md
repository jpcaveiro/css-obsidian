# Approximating integrals using the Ergodic Theorem

It means **replacing an integral by an average of values generated by a Markov chain**. More precisely, the average provides the approximation; the Ergodic Theorem justifies why that average converges to the desired integral. [python-advanced.quantecon](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/mcmc.html)

## From an integral to a simulated average

Suppose we want to calculate

$$
I=\int h(x)\,\pi(x)\,dx=\mathbb E_\pi[h(X)],
$$

where $\pi$ is a probability density. In MCMC, we simulate a Markov chain $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ with stationary distribution $\pi$, and estimate the integral using the average:[python-advanced.quantecon](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/mcmc.html)

$$
\boxed{\widehat I_N=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^{N}h(X_t).}
$$

Under suitable ergodicity conditions, and provided $\int |h(x)|\pi(x)\,dx<\infty$, the Ergodic Theorem gives the following convergence almost surely [python-advanced.quantecon](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/mcmc.html):

$$
\underbrace{\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^{N}h(X_t)}_{\text{average along one simulated trajectory}}
\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\text{a.s.}}\;
\underbrace{\int h(x)\pi(x)\,dx}_{\text{average under the target distribution}}.
$$

The important point is that **the simulated values need not be independent**. The theorem plays a role similar to the Law of Large Numbers, but applies to the dependent sequence produced by the Markov chain.[python-advanced.quantecon](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/mcmc.html)

## A concrete example

Suppose the desired integral is the second moment of the target distribution:

$$
I=\int x^2\pi(x)\,dx.
$$

Taking $h(x)=x^2$, the approximation is simply

$$
\widehat I_N=\frac{X_1^2+X_2^2+\cdots+X_N^2}{N}.
$$

For illustration, if a simulated run produced $1,2,1,0,1$, this estimate would be

$$
\widehat I_5=\frac{1+4+1+0+1}{5}=1.4.
$$

This is only a numerical illustration, not a claim that five iterations are enough for accuracy.

## What the phrase does not mean

The theorem guarantees long-run convergence, not that a particular finite simulation is already accurate. By itself, it does not tell us how many iterations are sufficient or give a finite-run error bound.[python-advanced.quantecon](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/mcmc.html)

So a clearer version of the original phrase is: **“The integrals are approximated by averages along a simulated Markov chain, with convergence justified by the Ergodic Theorem.”** This is the connection between the “Markov chain” and “Monte Carlo” parts of MCMC.[aaronstevenwhite](https://aaronstevenwhite.io/stats-in-ling/statistical-inference/markov-chain-monte-carlo.html)
